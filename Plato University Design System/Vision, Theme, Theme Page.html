The vision · 2026 — 2036

We are still running a school built for the factory floor.
We're building the one that comes after.

Plato University is a one-year academy for the AI era. Philosophy first. Portfolio last. It is small, city-rooted, unapologetically literary, and — for the right kind of mind — the most honest schooling available in the decade ahead.

A letter from the founder Amsterdam · Spring 2026

The modern university was built to produce a very particular kind of person: a reliable employee for the industrial economy. Rows, bells, grades, majors, and the Carnegie Unit — these aren't timeless features of education. They're nineteenth-century inventions, designed for a nineteenth-century problem.

That problem has been solved. The economy it was built for is already dissolving. What replaces it is not a new syllabus. It is a different shape.

We think the replacement looks less like the research university and more like Plato's Academy — a small community of people reading primary sources, asking hard questions, defending their work in front of one another, and walking home through a city they know by name.

The thesis, in four movements

i.

AI has made "how to do the job" the cheap part.

Within a decade, almost every technical skill that carries a premium today will be a commodity. What remains rare — and worth paying for — is the capacity to judge what is worth doing, to argue for it, and to make it well. That is not a technical skill. That is a philosophical one.

ii.

The answer is not more job-training. It is less.

Bootcamps, nanodegrees, and micro-credentials all double down on the premise the industrial university got wrong — that the point of school is employability. That premise was always fragile. AI just made it untenable.

iii.

Cities are the campus.

A university does not need a quad. It needs a room with chairs arranged in a circle, a seminar that meets weekly, and a city that can hold both. Amsterdam is hub one. Every city with five or more students becomes its own hub. This is not online school. This is online reading, in-person thinking.

iv.

We graduate thinkers with a portfolio and a defended capstone.

Not a diploma. Not a transcript. A body of work and a half-hour of hard questions survived in front of a room of mentors. We think this is a better credential than the one our institutions still sell. In five years we expect the market to agree.

Learn to think before you learn to work. The tagline, and the whole program in seven words.

What we are against · what we are for

We are against credentialism, debt, early specialization, and the Prussian bell. We are for the examined life, made portable.

The industrial university has an enemy: itself, eighty years on. Ours has an enemy too — the quiet assumption that a young person should spend a decade of their life buying the right to be employable. We reject it. We think you can build a better life, and a better body of work, in one year of serious reading and serious making, inside the city you already live in.

That is the whole bet.

— with conviction, the founding team Plato University · Amsterdam

The theme of the year —

Thinking in the
Age of the Machine.

What does it mean to have a mind, now that minds can be bought by the hour?

Every Plato University cohort is organized around a single question that runs through the whole year. The founding cohort asks the one we think the decade will demand an answer to.

i.
Trimester one

What is a mind?

Plato, Descartes, Ryle, Turing, Searle, Dennett. The oldest question, read cleanly, in the order it actually developed. We do not skip to the good part.

ii.
Trimester two

What is a machine?

From Babbage's engine to the transformer. Not a survey — a close reading of the four papers and two books that explain why our moment exists, and what it owes to the ones before it.

iii.
Trimester three

What is a life, now that the two can be confused?

The practical trimester. Every student ships a project defending an answer — an essay, a tool, a company, a piece of writing, a syllabus. Their capstone.

The twelve questions

Each seminar answers one.

i.Can a machine think? What would it take to know?
ii.Is the mind a kind of computation, or only sometimes?
iii.What is judgment, and can it be automated?
iv.Is language a tool we built, or a house we live in?
v.What does it mean to know something, versus to retrieve it?
vi.Who owns a thought produced with a machine's help?
vii.What is a good life when most labor becomes optional?
viii.Is education the transfer of skills, or the formation of a person?
ix.What does a city owe its young — and what do the young owe the city?
x.Should we trust a mind we did not raise?
xi.What is the honest work of a human being in 2036?
xii.Having read, argued, and built — what will you defend?

For the founding cohort of Plato University, we chose one. It runs through every seminar, every reading, every mentor conversation, and every capstone defense. You will leave with a defended answer.

Theme Thinking in the Age of the Machine Discipline Philosophy, close reading, practice Seats 30

Why this
question,
this year.

Most schools organize the year around a calendar. We organize it around a problem. The problem of the founding cohort is the one we think will define the decade: the quiet reshaping of what it means to have a mind, now that minds can be hired by the hour.

This is not a technology question. It is an older question — asked by Plato, Descartes, and Turing, each in the vocabulary of their own century. It's our turn.

You will not be taught the answer. You will be given the texts, the room, the mentors, and the year — and you will be asked to arrive at one, defend it, and live with the consequences.

How the theme moves

Three trimesters. Three moves. One argument.

Trimester I · Foundations 01

Read the mind.

Four months with the thinkers who defined what a mind is — from Plato's chariot to Turing's imitation game. Close reading, weekly seminars, short written arguments.

Trimester II · The world as it is 02

Read the machine.

Four months with the thinkers who explain our moment — Illich on tools, Kuhn on paradigms, Freire on learning, Sennett on craft. The books that make sense of the present.

Trimester III · Ship something 03

Defend an answer.

Four months to make the thing you will stake your year on — an essay, a tool, a company, a syllabus, a book. Defended publicly in front of the cohort and mentors.

Three lenses on the theme

We don't ask the question once. We ask it from three angles.

Philosophical

What kind of thing is a mind?

The metaphysics of thought. Consciousness, judgment, qualia, the mereology of mental life. We read the canon straight — no secondary literature, no shortcuts.

i.
Historical

How did we get here?

A close reading of the four papers and two books that explain how the transformer became a cultural event. Not a timeline — a genealogy. You will understand the machines, not just use them.

ii.
Practical

What should I do about it?

Every student answers this concretely. A thing built in public, defended in front of the cohort, and mentored by a working adult in the city you live in. This is the trimester that changes your career.

iii.
"The unexamined life is not worth living."
So examine one.
Socrates, and the syllabus.
